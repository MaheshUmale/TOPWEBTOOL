The 28/36 Rule for Home Buying

The 28/36 rule is the cornerstone of mortgage underwriting, providing a simple framework for determining responsible borrowing limits. Named for its two key ratios, the rule helps lenders and borrowers alike understand how much housing debt is manageable relative to income. While not a hard law, the 28/36 rule serves as a reliable benchmark for maintaining financial health while achieving homeownership.

Understanding the Front-End 28%

The front-end ratio limits monthly housing expenses to twenty-eight percent of gross monthly income. Housing expenses include principal, interest, property taxes, and homeowner's insurance, collectively known as PITI. For a borrower earning six thousand dollars monthly, the maximum housing payment under this rule is one thousand six hundred eighty dollars. This threshold ensures borrowers retain sufficient income for other living expenses and savings goals.

Understanding the Back-End 36%

The back-end ratio caps total monthly debt obligations at thirty-six percent of gross monthly income. This includes the proposed housing payment plus all existing debts such as car loans, student loans, credit card minimums, and personal loans. Using the same six thousand dollar monthly income, total debt payments should not exceed two thousand one hundred sixty dollars. The back-end ratio provides a comprehensive view of your debt load and protects against overextension.

Exceptions and Flexibility

Lenders recognize that rigid ratios do not fit every borrower. Automated underwriting systems may approve loans with front-end ratios up to thirty-one percent and back-end ratios up to forty-three percent for borrowers with strong credit, large down payments, or substantial cash reserves. FHA loans sometimes stretch back-end DTI to fifty percent for well-qualified applicants. VA loans focus on residual income rather than strict DTI caps, allowing veterans with higher debt loads to qualify if they demonstrate sufficient income after paying all bills.

How Lenders Calculate DTI

Lenders pull your credit report to identify monthly debt obligations. They calculate housing expenses using the proposed loan amount, interest rate, property taxes, and insurance. For variable debts like credit cards, they use the minimum required payment rather than the full balance. Student loans may be calculated using the actual payment or a percentage of the balance, depending on the loan program. Always ask your lender which method they use for each debt type to ensure accurate expectations.

Planning Within the 28/36 Rule

Use the 28/36 rule to plan your homebuying timeline and budget. Calculate your maximum qualifying payment based on current income and debts. Research homes in your target market that fit within that payment range, accounting for down payment savings and closing costs. If your target home exceeds the 28/36 threshold, consider strategies such as increasing your down payment, paying down debt, or waiting for income growth. Common Mistakes to Avoid

Do not stretch to the absolute limit of the 28/36 rule, as unexpected expenses can quickly push you over. Maintain a buffer for repairs, maintenance, and lifestyle needs. Avoid taking on new debt before closing, as it can change your DTI at the worst possible moment. Remember that being approved for a certain loan amount does not mean you should borrow the maximum. Borrowing less provides flexibility, reduces stress, and builds wealth faster.

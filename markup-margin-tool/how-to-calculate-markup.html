How to Calculate Markup: Step-by-Step Cost-Plus Pricing Guide

Last updated: August 2026 • Read time: 7 min

Setting the right price for your products is one of the most challenging parts of running a business. Price too high and you lose customers. Price too low and you leave money on the table or even lose money on every sale. The markup formula is one of the simplest and most reliable methods for pricing products, yet many entrepreneurs still calculate it incorrectly or skip the math entirely. This guide explains exactly how to calculate markup, how to apply cost-plus pricing in real-world scenarios, and how to verify your numbers with the /markup-margin-tool.

What Is the Markup Formula?

Markup is calculated by taking the difference between the selling price and the cost price, then dividing that difference by the cost price. The result is expressed as a percentage. The basic markup formula is:

Markup Percentage = ((Selling Price - Cost) / Cost) x 100

For example, if you purchase inventory for $40 and sell it for $60, the markup is ((60 - 40) / 40) x 100 = 50 percent. This means you added half of the cost value to arrive at the final price. The markup formula is straightforward, but applying it consistently across hundreds of SKUs requires discipline and organization.

How to Calculate Markup in Four Steps

Whether you are pricing a single handmade craft item or an entire product catalog, follow these steps to calculate markup accurately.

  1. Determine your total cost. Include the purchase price, shipping, taxes, packaging, and any labor directly tied to producing the item. Hidden costs are where many businesses lose money.
  2. Choose your target markup percentage. This depends on your industry, competition, and overhead. Retailers often use 50 to 150 percent markup, while restaurants may use 200 to 300 percent on food.
  3. Apply the markup formula. Multiply your cost by the markup percentage and add the result to the original cost. For a $40 item with a 50 percent markup, the sale price is $40 + ($40 x 0.50) = $60.
  4. Verify the final margin. Convert the markup back to margin to ensure the profit level meets your needs. In this example, a 50 percent markup yields a 33.3 percent margin.

Cost-Plus Pricing Explained

Cost-plus pricing is the strategy of adding a fixed markup to the cost of producing or acquiring a product. It is popular among retailers, manufacturers, and contractors because it guarantees that each sale covers costs and contributes to profit. The main advantage is simplicity. You do not need complex market research to set an initial price; you simply cover costs and add your desired return.

However, cost-plus pricing has limitations. It does not account for what customers are willing to pay or what competitors charge. If your costs rise but you keep the same markup, your margin shrinks. If your costs fall, your margin expands even if you keep prices unchanged. That is why many businesses combine cost-plus pricing with competitive and value-based pricing for different product lines.

Markup Examples Across Industries

Markup percentages vary widely by industry. Grocery stores often operate on 10 to 15 percent markup, which sounds low but generates profit through high volume. Clothing retailers may use 100 to 250 percent markup on apparel. Restaurants typically apply 200 to 300 percent markup on raw ingredients, though the actual profit margin is lower after accounting for labor, rent, and utilities. Electronics retailers often see 5 to 10 percent markup on hardware because products are commoditized.

Common Markup Calculation Mistakes

The most frequent error is confusing markup with margin. As discussed in our guide on markup vs margin, a 50 percent markup does not equal a 50 percent margin. Another mistake is forgetting variable costs. If you only consider the purchase price and ignore shipping, returns, or payment processing fees, your actual margin will be lower than expected.

  • Ignoring overhead: Rent, utilities, and salaries must eventually be covered by margin, not just markup.
  • Using retail price as cost: If you source products from a distributor who already marked up the item, your real cost is the distributor price, not the manufacturer price.
  • Static markup across products: Some items deserve higher markup due to uniqueness or low competition, while others need lower markup to stay competitive.
  • Rounding errors: Small rounding differences compound across hundreds of units and significantly affect annual profit.

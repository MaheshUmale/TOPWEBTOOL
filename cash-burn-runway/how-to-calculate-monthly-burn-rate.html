How to Calculate Monthly Burn Rate: Step-by-Step Guide

Monthly burn rate is one of the most closely watched metrics in startup finance. It measures the net amount of cash a company loses each month and directly determines how long the business can survive without additional funding. Calculating burn rate accurately requires separating revenue from expenses, understanding the difference between gross and net burn, and projecting future trends based on realistic assumptions. This guide walks you through every step.

Step 1: Identify All Monthly Revenue Streams

Before calculating burn, you need a complete picture of monthly revenue. Include recurring revenue from subscriptions, one-time sales, service fees, and any other income streams. Use averaged figures if revenue fluctuates significantly month to month. Be conservative: understate revenue rather than overstate it. Many startups make the mistake of counting pending invoices or signed contracts that have not yet converted to cash. Only include cash that has been received or is highly likely to be received within the standard payment terms.

Step 2: Total Monthly Operating Expenses

Operating expenses include salaries, rent, software subscriptions, marketing spend, legal fees, and any other costs required to keep the business running. Do not include capital expenditures such as equipment purchases or long-term asset acquisitions in your monthly burn calculation, as these are typically one-time or depreciable items. Categorize expenses into fixed and variable components. Fixed costs remain constant regardless of revenue, while variable costs scale with business activity. Understanding this distinction helps you model how burn changes as the company grows.

Step 3: Calculate Gross Burn Rate

Gross burn rate is simply the total monthly operating expenses before accounting for revenue. It answers the question: "How much cash do we spend each month?" To calculate it, sum all monthly operating expenses. Gross burn is useful for understanding cost structure and for comparing efficiency across similar businesses. However, gross burn does not account for revenue, so it cannot tell you how long your cash will last. That is where net burn comes in.

Step 4: Calculate Net Burn Rate

Net burn rate equals monthly operating expenses minus monthly revenue. If your company spends one hundred fifty thousand dollars per month and generates eighty thousand dollars in revenue, your net burn is seventy thousand dollars. Net burn is the metric that matters for runway calculations because it represents the actual monthly cash deficit. Positive net burn means you are spending more than you earn, which is typical for early-stage startups. Negative net burn means you are profitable, which extends runway indefinitely.

Step 5: Incorporate Revenue Growth

Static burn rate calculations assume revenue and expenses remain flat, but startups rarely operate that way. If your revenue is growing monthly, your effective burn rate declines over time because revenue offsets more of your expenses. You can model this by applying a growth rate to monthly revenue and recalculating net burn for each projected month. For example, if revenue grows five percent monthly, a startup starting with seventy-five thousand dollars in revenue will see that figure rise to nearly one hundred fourteen thousand dollars after twelve months of compound growth. This growth dramatically reduces net burn and extends runway.

Step 6: Factor in Variable Costs and Seasonality

Some expenses vary by season, such as holiday marketing spend or annual software renewals. Account for these fluctuations by using a three-month or six-month average rather than a single month's data. Similarly, variable costs such as cloud hosting or transaction fees should be estimated based on projected growth. Step 7: Validate and Monitor Regularly

Burn rate calculations are only as good as the data behind them. Recalculate monthly using actual financial statements rather than estimates. Compare projected burn to actual burn and adjust your forecasts accordingly. Keep a rolling thirteen-week cash flow forecast to identify shortfalls before they become crises. Share burn rate updates with your board and investors to maintain transparency and trust. Consistent, accurate burn rate tracking is the foundation of sound financial management for any startup.

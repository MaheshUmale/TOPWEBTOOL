How to Use the W-2 vs 1099 Compensation Comparator

Compares take-home and true cost between employee (W-2) and contractor (1099) pay — the same headline number hides very different economics.

  1. Enter the W-2 salary with typical benefits (health, retirement match, payroll taxes).
  2. Enter the 1099 rate and hours — contractors cover their own FICA, health, and retirement.
  3. Compare take-home, true hourly value, and the rate a contractor needs to match the employee package.

Why a 1099 Rate Must Be Higher (and How Much)

The 1099 breakeven: contractors pay both halves of FICA (15.3% vs. the employee's 7.65% — though half is deductible), plus health insurance, retirement match, paid leave, and unemployment/workers-comp coverage they lack. Worked example at $80,000: the W-2 employee's true employer cost is ~$92,000–$100,000 with normal benefits; a contractor matching that package needs roughly $95,000–$110,000 pre-expense — a 20–35% premium over the salaried number. Rule of thumb circulating among freelancers (W-2 hourly × 1.3–1.5) exists for a reason. Misclassification is the compliance edge: the IRS common-law tests (behavioral control, financial control, relationship) decide the label, not preference — penalties fund the enforcement.

W-2 vs 1099 Compensation Comparator FAQ

What 1099 rate equals a $100k salary?

Roughly $125k-$140k depending on health costs and benefits replaced - the contractor pays both FICA halves plus benefits the employer used to cover.

Why do contractors pay more tax?

Self-employment tax: the full 15.3% FICA instead of half. The deductible half and business expense write-offs soften but do not erase it.

Can my employer just make me a 1099 contractor?

Only if the work relationship genuinely passes IRS common-law tests. Same hours, same control, same tools = employee, and misclassification penalties are severe.

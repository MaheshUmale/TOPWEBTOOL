Common Errors in Pregnancy Due Date Prediction

Published: August 2026 • Category: Everyday & Health • No Sign-Up / 100% Free / No Registration

Educational estimate only � not medical/insurance advice. Consult a qualified professional.

The due date is one of the most googled numbers in prenatal life, yet it is also one of the most frequently misunderstood. Most of the confusion is not about the math � adding 280 days is trivial � but about the assumptions and conventions that surround it. Knowing the common errors helps you avoid planning a birth, a scan, or a leave around a number that means something other than you think it does.

The most basic error is confusing conception with the last menstrual period. Gestational age is measured from the LMP, which is roughly two weeks before fertilization. A woman who counts her pregnancy from the date she conceived will believe she is two weeks further along than standard dating says, which throws off everything from the timing of the first scan to the expected due date itself. The calculator's two modes make the distinction explicit: LMP gets 280 days, conception gets 266, and the two differ by exactly the 14-day ovulation assumption.

Ignoring cycle length is the second classic mistake. Naegele's rule presumes a 28-day cycle, and for anyone with a longer or shorter cycle the estimate drifts. With a 35-day cycle, ovulation commonly happens around day 21, meaning the LMP-based date is roughly a week earlier than reality. Many couples shrug this off, but a week's difference can matter for the timing of the nuchal translucency scan, which has a narrow window. If your cycles are not 28 days, say so and prefer an early ultrasound.

A subtler failure is entering the wrong day of the LMP. People recall "early last month" and pick an arbitrary date, or they enter the day bleeding stopped. Because the whole estimate pivots on one calendar day, a five-day recall error is a five-day due-date error. The fix is discipline: record the first day of each period as it happens. A calendar app that logs cycle starts turns a vague memory into a precise input.

Treating the due date as a delivery deadline creates unnecessary stress and, occasionally, unwise interventions. The "40 weeks" figure is a statistical mode, not a promise; only about 5 percent of babies are born on their exact due date, and the normal range spans 37 to 42 weeks. Every week of that range is normal. Planning as if the due date is a hard deadline � canceling travel, scheduling inductions on anxiety alone � is a behavioral error born from a good estimate used for the wrong purpose.

Weeks-versus-months confusion is nearly universal and perfectly understandable, because pregnancy months do not align with calendar months. "Five months pregnant" is 20-ish weeks, "seven months" is around 28 to 31 weeks, and trimesters split at 13 and 28 weeks, not at neat three-month calendar boundaries. The mistake surfaces when people count forward month by month from the LMP and arrive at a "due month" that disagrees with the weeks shown on the calculator. Trust the weeks; they are the medical standard.

Rounding gestational age instead of completing it is another quiet error. "16 weeks 6 days" rounded to 17 weeks makes a difference when a screening window closes at the end of a particular week. Obstetrics counts completed weeks � you are 16 weeks 6 days, not 17 weeks, until the next day begins. The calculator displays completed weeks and days precisely, and the milestone statuses reflect exactly that threshold logic.

Some users mix up the two dating routes mid-conversation, computing the EDD one way with their provider and another way at home. Since conception-based dating subtracts the 14-day assumption and LMP dating adds 280, mixing them produces two different dates and needless alarm when they differ. Pick one consistent reference � ideally the LMP your care team is using � and keep it the same in every calculator and every conversation.

Finally, ignoring the ultrasound adjustment is an error of stubbornness. If a first-trimester scan yields a date several days different from the LMP-based estimate, the ultrasound date is generally more reliable, because it measures the baby rather than assuming the calendar. Continuing to plan around the older, less accurate date when a better one is available is a common and easily fixed mistake.

How to Calculate Rental Yield

Rental yield is one of the most fundamental metrics for evaluating investment properties. It measures the annual rental income relative to the property's value or purchase price, giving investors a quick sense of income potential. Higher yields generally indicate better cash flow opportunities, though they may also reflect higher risk or less desirable locations. Learning to calculate both gross and net rental yield helps you screen properties efficiently and compare opportunities across different markets.

Gross Rental Yield Formula

Gross rental yield is the simplest metric to calculate. Divide the annual rental income by the property purchase price and multiply by one hundred to express it as a percentage. For example, a property purchased for three hundred thousand dollars that rents for twenty-five hundred dollars monthly generates thirty thousand dollars annually, yielding a gross yield of ten percent. This calculation requires only two inputs and can be performed in seconds, making it ideal for initial property screening.

Net Rental Yield Formula

Net rental yield provides a more accurate picture by subtracting operating expenses from rental income before dividing by the purchase price. Operating expenses include property taxes, insurance, maintenance, property management fees, HOA dues, and estimated vacancy. A property with a ten percent gross yield might produce a six percent net yield after expenses. Always calculate net yield before making investment decisions, as gross yield alone can be deeply misleading.

What is a Good Rental Yield?

Target yields vary by market, property type, and investor strategy. In major metropolitan areas like New York or San Francisco, gross yields often fall between three and five percent due to high property values. Midwestern and Southern markets frequently offer six to ten percent gross yields on similar properties. Class A assets in prime locations yield less but appreciate more steadily, while Class C properties in secondary markets offer higher yields but greater risk. Compare yields against local market averages rather than national benchmarks.

Factors That Affect Rental Yield

Purchase price, rental rates, vacancy, and operating expenses all influence yield. High property values compress yield, while below-market rents or excessive expenses reduce net yield. Value-add strategies such as renovating units, converting spaces, or improving management can boost yield significantly. Some investors accept lower initial yields in exchange for strong appreciation potential in growing markets. Rental Yield Versus Total Return

Yield measures current income, while total return includes appreciation, equity buildup, and tax benefits. A property with a modest five percent yield might still outperform a higher-yielding property in a declining market. Evaluate yield alongside cap rates, cash-on-cash return, and projected appreciation to build a complete investment thesis. The best properties often balance moderate yield with strong growth potential and favorable financing terms.

Common Rental Yield Mistakes

Beginning investors often use seller-provided rent estimates instead of verified market rents, inflating gross yield unrealistically. Others forget to account for vacancy and maintenance, producing net yield figures that are impossible to achieve. Some investors ignore property management costs, especially if they plan to self-manage, only to discover the time commitment is far greater than expected. Always use conservative, verifiable assumptions when calculating yield, and build a cushion for unexpected expenses.

Using Rental Yield in Investment Analysis

Rental yield is most powerful as a screening tool. Calculate gross yield for every listing in your target market to identify candidates worth deeper analysis.

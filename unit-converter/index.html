The Factor-Label Method (and the Temperature Exception)

5 km × (1000 m / 1 km) = 5000 m  |  °C→°F: ×9/5 + 32

Nearly all unit conversion is multiplying by ratios that cancel: 5 km × (1000 m / 1 km) = 5,000 m — the "factor-label" method students use in chemistry, executed instantly across every field here. The exception everyone memorizes wrong: temperature scales have offsets, not ratios — °C→°F is ×9/5 + 32 (0°C is 32°F, not 0°F), while temperature differences convert ratio-only (a 10°C rise = 18°F rise, no offset). Magnitude anchors worth internalizing: a meter ≈ a long stride; a liter of water = 1 kg; a US gallon = 3.785 L; a mile = 1.609 km; 1 psi ≈ 6.895 kPa. Between imperial and metric, exact definitions exist (1 inch = 25.4 mm exactly by treaty since 1959) — conversions are arithmetic, not folklore.

Unit Converter FAQ

Why is temperature conversion different?

Scales have offsets: °C to °F multiplies by 9/5 then adds 32. Differences convert without the offset - a 10°C change is an 18°F change.

How accurate are these conversions?

Exact by international definition - the inch is defined as exactly 25.4 mm. Only historical/commercial units carry tiny agreed-roundings.

What is the factor-label method?

Chain ratios so units cancel: km x (m/km) x (cm/m). It is the systematic way to convert anything and catch dimensional mistakes.

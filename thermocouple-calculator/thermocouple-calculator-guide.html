The Complete Guide to Thermocouple Millivolt Conversion

Published: August 2026 • Category: Industrial & Engineering • No Sign-Up / 100% Free / No Registration

A thermocouple is the simplest temperature sensor in industrial use: two wires of different alloys joined at one end, producing a small voltage that rises with temperature. But that apparent simplicity hides a subtlety that catches every newcomer — the voltage you measure is not simply the temperature of the probe. It depends on the temperature at the other end of the wires too. This guide explains the physics, the reference standard, and the compensation that makes the Thermocouple Calculator's conversions trustworthy.

The phenomenon at the heart of the thermocouple is the Seebeck effect. When two different metals are joined and the two junctions are held at different temperatures, a voltage develops in the circuit that is proportional, to a very close approximation, to the temperature difference between the junctions. One junction is the measuring junction at the tip of the probe, and the other is the reference junction where the two thermocouple wires connect to the copper leads of the instrument. The measured voltage is therefore the difference between the two junction temperatures — which is why the reference junction must be known for the reading to mean anything.

The international reference scale is the ITS-90, and the defining equations for thermocouples are the NIST polynomials that convert between temperature and millivoltage for each thermocouple type. Each type — J, K, T, and the others — has its own set of polynomial coefficients, valid over its specific temperature range, and each is calibrated against the ITS-90 temperature scale. The Type K polynomial, for example, is split into a sub-zero range and an above-zero range, the latter including a small exponential correction term, and together they cover −270 to 1372 °C. The calculator embeds these exact coefficients, so the conversions it performs are the same ones a calibration laboratory uses.

The three types on this page cover the overwhelming majority of industrial measurement. Type K, with nickel-chromium and nickel-aluminium alloys, is the general-purpose workhorse, spanning −270 to 1372 °C with a sensitivity near 41 µV per degree. Type J, iron against constantan, is the economy choice for lower-temperature work and delivers the highest sensitivity of the three at roughly 51 µV per degree, but its iron leg limits it to about 1200 °C and makes it vulnerable to oxidation. Type T, copper against constantan, is prized for low temperatures — down to −270 °C — and is highly stable in clean, dry environments, but its copper leg caps it near 400 °C. The reference table on the calculator's hub page lists each type's range and its nominal signal level at 100 °C so the right type can be chosen at a glance.

Conversion from millivolts to temperature is the direction that most field measurements need. The instrument measures a voltage E at its terminals, and the measured temperature is the temperature whose reference function produces that voltage — but only if the reference junction is at 0 °C, the ice point, which is how the NIST polynomials are defined. When the reference junction sits at some other temperature, the reading must be corrected by adding the EMF that the thermocouple would generate at the reference temperature, then converting the total: T = (E_measured + E_reference)⁻¹. This is cold-junction compensation, and the calculator applies it explicitly, printing both the measured EMF and the reference EMF in the breakdown.

Converting the other way, from temperature to millivolts, is the inverse operation and is the one used in calibration. A laboratory sets a known temperature, computes the expected EMF from the NIST polynomial, and compares it with what the instrument reads. Because the ITS-90 polynomials are invertible over their valid ranges, the calculator performs both directions: it evaluates the forward polynomial E = Σ cᵢ Tⁱ for the temperature-to-mV direction, and for the reverse it solves the polynomial by iteration, finding the temperature whose EMF matches the input to within a fraction of a millivolt.

The range limits matter as much as the coefficients. A Type K reading of 60 mV is impossible — the polynomial simply does not reach it — and the calculator rejects out-of-range signals instead of extrapolating, because a number produced by extrapolation is not a measurement. The valid range of each type, and its maximum EMF, are part of the embedded data, and the calculator's warnings steer the user back into range when a voltage or temperature exceeds them. The same discipline applies to the reference junction: a reference temperature outside the type's range is rejected, because the compensation EMF would be undefined.

For the engineer on the floor, the practical workflow is short. Read the millivolts from the instrument, note the reference junction temperature (0 °C at the ice point, otherwise the temperature at the instrument terminals), select the thermocouple type, and let the calculator return the temperature with the compensation shown. In calibration, enter the temperature and read the expected EMF. In both directions the calculator prints the substituted values, so the conversion can be audited the way a calibration record demands — every term visible, every unit explicit, and every constant traceable to the NIST reference data it embeds.

The millivolt conversion is the mathematics under all temperature measurement with thermocouples, and getting it right separates a trustworthy reading from a plausible one. The ITS-90 polynomials, the cold-junction compensation, and the range checks are the three pillars of that trust, and the calculator on this page puts all three within reach of anyone with a voltmeter and a probe.

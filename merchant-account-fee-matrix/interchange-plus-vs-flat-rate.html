Interchange Plus vs Flat Rate: Which is Better for Your Business?

Choosing the right credit card processing pricing model is one of the most important financial decisions a small business owner can make. The two dominant options in the market are flat rate pricing and interchange-plus pricing. While both accomplish the same goal of facilitating card transactions, they differ significantly in structure, transparency, and potential cost savings. Understanding these differences can mean the difference between paying thousands of dollars in unnecessary fees or redirecting that capital toward growth initiatives, inventory, and staffing.

Flat rate pricing is the simplest model to understand. Processors like Square, PayPal, and Stripe typically charge a single percentage of each transaction plus a fixed fee, such as 2.9% plus $0.30 per swipe. There are no hidden costs, no monthly statements to decode, and no surprises. For very small businesses or seasonal operations with low transaction volumes, flat rate pricing offers predictability and ease of use that is hard to match. The tradeoff is that the single rate is often higher than what larger merchants pay, because the processor bundles interchange fees, assessment fees, and their own markup into one all-inclusive price.

Interchange-plus pricing, sometimes called cost-plus pricing, separates the costs into three distinct components: the interchange fee set by card networks, the assessment fee charged by Visa or Mastercard, and the processor's markup. This transparency is the model's greatest strength. A typical interchange-plus rate might look like interchange plus 0.15% plus $0.10 per transaction. Because interchange fees vary by card type—reward cards cost more to process than standard debit cards—interchange-plus pricing can actually save businesses money when customers use lower-cost payment methods. For businesses with consistent monthly volumes above ten thousand dollars, interchange-plus almost always beats flat rate pricing over time.

The break-even point between the two models depends heavily on average ticket size and monthly transaction count. A coffee shop with a fifteen-dollar average ticket and five hundred monthly transactions may find flat rate pricing simpler and cheaper. A business consulting firm with a one-thousand-dollar average ticket and two hundred monthly transactions may save hundreds of dollars per month by switching to interchange-plus. Beyond pure cost, there are operational considerations to weigh. Flat rate processors usually offer same-day or next-day deposits, no long-term contracts, and integrated point-of-sale systems. Interchange-plus processors often require a traditional merchant account, which can involve credit checks, setup fees, and longer contracts. However, interchange-plus customers typically enjoy lower overall costs, dedicated account support, and the ability to negotiate rates as their volume grows. For any business serious about optimizing its payment infrastructure, the question is not whether to accept credit cards—it is which pricing structure aligns with revenue patterns and long-term financial goals.

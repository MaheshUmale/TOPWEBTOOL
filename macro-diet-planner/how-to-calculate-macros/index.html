How to Calculate Macros: The Complete Step-by-Step Guide for Weight Loss & Muscle Gain

Published: August 2026 • Category: Nutrition & Macro Planning • No Sign-Up / 100% Free / No Registration

Calculating your own macros by hand used to require a scientific calculator and a lot of patience with unit conversions. The good news is that the math itself is simple once you know the four steps: find your Basal Metabolic Rate, multiply by an activity factor to get TDEE, apply a goal adjustment, and then convert calories into grams using the fixed calorie values of each macronutrient. The Macro Diet Planner at /macro-diet-planner runs all of this automatically, but understanding each step means you can audit your results and know exactly why the numbers look the way they do.

Step 1 — Estimate Your Basal Metabolic Rate

Your BMR is the number of calories your body burns at complete rest in a 24-hour period. The Mifflin-St Jeor equation is the most accurate population-level formula available without expensive lab testing, and it is what the calculator at /macro-diet-planner uses under the hood:

  • Men: BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) + 5
  • Women: BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) − 161

If you live outside the metric system, convert pounds to kilograms by dividing by 2.2046 and inches to centimeters by multiplying by 2.54. One common mistake is forgetting to convert before plugging numbers in, which silently inflates every downstream result.

Step 2 — Apply Your Activity Factor to Get TDEE

BMR only covers rest. Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE) multiplies BMR by how active you are. The honest part is picking the right multiplier: most people overestimate their own activity level. Use the table below as a starting point, then adjust after two weeks of data: /macro-diet-planner ships with these presets so you do not have to remember them.

  1. Sedentary (×1.2): little or no exercise, desk job.
  2. Light (×1.375): light exercise 1–3 days per week.
  3. Moderate (×1.55): moderate exercise 3–5 days per week.
  4. Very active (×1.725): hard exercise 6–7 days per week.
  5. Athlete (×1.9): twice-daily or elite-level training.

Step 3 — Set Your Goal Adjustment

TDEE is the number of calories that should keep your weight stable. To lose weight or gain mass you create a deficit or surplus on top of it. A deficit that is too large threatens muscle retention and performance, so the safer ranges are:

  • Weight loss: subtract 15–25% from TDEE (roughly a 500–750 kcal/day deficit).
  • Maintenance: use TDEE exactly.
  • Muscle gain: add 10–15% above TDEE (a 300–500 kcal/day surplus).

Step 4 — Convert Calories to Grams

This is where the 4/4/9 system comes in. Protein and carbohydrate each provide 4 calories per gram, and fat provides 9 calories per gram. The usual order is to set protein first based on lean mass, set fat as a minimum floor, and let carbohydrates take whatever calories remain:

  1. Protein grams = 1.6–2.2 g per kg of lean body mass, then multiply by 4 for calories.
  2. Fat grams = 20–35% of target calories divided by 9.
  3. Carbohydrate grams = (target calories − protein calories − fat calories) ÷ 4.

If the result for carbohydrates is negative, the protein-plus-fat share has already exceeded your calorie target. In that case the only correct move is to lower the fat percentage or raise the calorie goal — and that is exactly the warning the tool at macro-diet-planner /macro-diet-planner surfaces as a notice instead of a bogus negative number. After the daily targets are set, divide each gram total by the number of meals you plan to eat to get a per-meal breakdown without doing any extra arithmetic.

Common Calculation Mistakes

Even with the correct formula, three errors silently derail most first attempts at macro math. First is unit confusion: entering pounds into a kilogram-based equation multiplies every result by ~2.2. Second is activity inflation: picking "very active" when you train three times a week overstates TDEE by 300–500 kcal. Third is using total body weight instead of lean body mass for the protein target, which can overshoot needs by 20–40% for higher-body-fat individuals.

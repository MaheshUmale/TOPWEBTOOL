Room Mode Physics & Room-Ratio Reference

Understand standing-wave resonance, the three mode families, and the recommended dimension ratios that avoid clustered modes.

How The Formulas Work

In a rectangular room, sound reflects off all six boundaries. At specific frequencies those reflections align to form standing waves, and the room "rings" — those are room modes. The resonance frequency of a mode with indices (nx, ny, nz) is f = (c/2) × √((nx/Lx)² + (ny/Ly)² + (nz/Lz)²), where c is the speed of sound and Lx, Ly, Lz are the room dimensions. When exactly one index is non-zero the mode is axial (bounces between one pair of opposite walls); two non-zero indices give a tangential mode; three give an oblique mode.

Why Ratios Matter

When two dimensions are equal or simple multiples, different index combinations produce identical (coincident) frequencies. A 1:1:1 cube clusters every mode into a few frequencies, creating severe bass peaks and nulls. Well-spread ratios such as the Bolt area (1 : 1.14 : 1.39) or Louden ratios (1 : 1.26 : 1.59) distribute the modes evenly across the spectrum, which makes the low end far easier to treat. The tool scales each standard ratio to match the current shortest side so you can compare directly.

Standard Ratio
Cube (worst) 1 : 1 : 1

Troubleshooting & Edge-Case Failure Points

  • Coincident modes: equal dimensions or 2:1 ratios make different index combinations share a frequency. The analyzer flags every mode in the problematic range so clustered resonances are visible at a glance.
  • Unit mismatch: entering feet into a meter formula shifts every frequency by a factor of 3.28. This tool sets the speed of sound to match the unit selector, so keep the selector in sync with your measurements.
  • Irregular rooms: the rectangular formula is exact only for rigid, rectangular enclosures. Angled walls, openings, and non-rigid boundaries shift the real resonances — use the result as a planning guide, not a final measurement.
  • Mode count is not bass response: a dense mode list below 200 Hz can indicate good modal distribution or a room full of problematic resonances. Use the flag column and the ratio match, not the raw count, to judge the room.
  • Speed of sound varies with temperature and humidity: 343 m/s assumes ~20 °C. Cold rooms push frequencies slightly lower; hot rooms slightly higher.

Step-by-Step Instructions

  1. Select the dimension unit (meters or feet) matching your measurements.
  2. Enter the room length Lx, width Ly, and height Lz.
  3. Set the upper limit of the problematic low-frequency range (default 200 Hz) and the number of modes to display.
  4. Click "Analyze Room Modes" to list the lowest modes sorted by frequency with type and flag status.
  5. Read the fundamental axial modes, flagged-mode count, modal density, and volume.
  6. Compare your ratios against the recommended standards, then copy the full analysis for your treatment plan.
